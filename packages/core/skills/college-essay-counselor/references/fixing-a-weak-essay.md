# How to Fix a Bad College Essay

*This article is adapted from a widely-discussed post by a private admissions consultant on r/ApplyingToCollege, originally published roughly six years ago. It draws on both the original post and the pushback and additions that came from other admissions professionals and applicants in the comments. Because it's several years old, some of the specific claims are worth a second look — those are called out explicitly toward the end.*

## Why Most First Drafts Fall Flat

Essay writing is an art, not a science. And like any art, most first attempts by people without much practice aren't great — that's normal, not a sign of failure. Ask an untrained person to draw a portrait and it usually won't look like much. The same is true for most students the first time they're asked to write expressively about themselves.

Part of the problem is that years of academic writing instruction work against personal essays. Students are trained to write formally and objectively, which quietly undermines the voice and personality a college essay actually needs.

That training shows up in the tropes. Students get too focused on sounding *impressive* rather than *expressive*. A sob story won't earn sympathy admission. Showing off intelligence won't add anything — the transcript and test scores already do that. Straining to be "unique" usually just reads as forced. The better move, almost always, is to just be yourself.

Experienced readers and admissions consultants converge on the same rough estimate: truly outstanding essays — the 9s and 10s on a ten-point scale — make up somewhere between one and five out of every hundred submissions. A useful rule of thumb: take a student's self-rating out of ten and subtract four to get something closer to the real score. Most students rate their essay a 7 or 8; it's often closer to a 3 or 4.

This isn't a reason to panic. An essay doesn't need to be museum-quality to work — but in an admissions environment this competitive, it does need to add something genuine to the application. Most other applicants' essays aren't great either, and most of them get rejected from highly selective schools. Standing out doesn't require perfection, just real improvement over the default.

Telling someone their essay isn't working is easy. Diagnosing exactly *what's* wrong is harder. Explaining how to fix it is hardest of all — which is the point of what follows: four common problems, and concrete ways to address each one.

One caveat worth stating up front, echoed by other readers in the original discussion: **essay evaluation is inherently subjective, and no single piece of feedback is gospel.** One commenter described sending their finished essay to two experienced reviewers — one called it "decent," the other called it "trash." The writer kept it largely as written, was admitted to Stanford, and the admissions officer specifically praised the essay. The lesson isn't that outside feedback is worthless — it's that any one reviewer's opinion is a data point, not a verdict, and it's evaluated alongside the rest of the application, not in a vacuum. Advice like this is meant to catch common, fixable problems — not to serve as a rigid formula every essay must follow.

## Problem 1: The Topic Doesn't Work

Two categories of topics tend to sink an essay before it starts:

- **Overused topics** — sports, mission trips, favorite foods, video games, moving to a new city.
- **Nearly impossible topics** — death, divorce, depression, drugs, sex, or writing *about* the college admissions process itself.

### How to Fix It

For overused topics, there are two paths forward: write one of the best essays ever written on that subject, or zoom in until it becomes something more personal. Don't write about the mission trip — write about one relationship formed during it, and let that relationship reveal who you are. Don't write about the championship won or lost — a hundred essays already exist about sports teaching teamwork and perseverance, and almost none of them are memorable.

One way to find a fresh angle: take a common topic and pair it with a core value that doesn't obviously relate to it. A soccer essay about imperialism stands out precisely because that combination is unexpected. If no fresh angle emerges after real effort, the honest move is to abandon the topic and start over.

For the "nearly impossible" topics — death, depression, trauma, and the like — there's rarely a way to save them. These subjects are so difficult to handle well, in such a short space, that the safest and most reliable choice is to pick a different topic entirely.

It's also worth noting that "overused" doesn't mean "off-limits." In the original discussion, another reviewer expanded on this using two examples: leadership and studying/living abroad. Nearly every applicant claims some version of these, but the fix isn't to avoid the topic — it's to define it on your own terms. What does "good leadership" specifically mean to *you*, based on something you actually did? What, concretely, did living abroad change about how you see something? Even a supposed failure — thinking you were a good leader or listener and later realizing you'd gotten it wrong — can make for a stronger essay than a clean success story, as long as it's honest and specific.

## Problem 2: The Essay Doesn't Say Much About the Writer

A quick diagnostic: read each paragraph and ask what it reveals about *you*, specifically. If a paragraph doesn't tell the reader something personal, it's probably too long — or doesn't belong at all.

Then try this: after finishing the essay, write down everything a reader might learn about you from it. If that list has fewer than three real items, or if generating even those feels like a stretch, that's a clear sign of the problem.

### How to Fix It

Cut the sections that don't reveal anything personal. Expand the sections that dig into real values, strengths, and motivations. Before revising, make an explicit list of what you want a reader to walk away knowing about you — then brainstorm specific examples, anecdotes, and relationships that could demonstrate each item on that list.

## Problem 3: Claims Without Support

Look for places in the essay that simply assert something about the writer without backing it up. Saying "I'm a creative problem solver" carries no more weight than a stranger online claiming expertise with nothing to show for it. No one believes a claim like that without evidence — an example, a story, a specific moment.

Watch especially for lines that are stated too directly, lean on clichés, or sound suspiciously polished and universal — the kind of sentence that could have been written by anyone. "I discovered that collaboration is the key to unlocking positive impact in the world" is the kind of line that makes a reader wince, not connect.

### How to Fix It

Replace assertions with evidence. Show the moment a trait was developed or demonstrated, rather than naming the trait and asking the reader to take it on faith. Swap clichés for original phrasing, and swap generalities for specific, concrete detail.

Also worth checking: sentences that use a vague "you" or "we" instead of "I." Compare "It can be hard to tell when you should just give up" with "I can never tell when I should just give up." The second version is more honest, more specific, and much harder to write generically — which is exactly why it's stronger.

## Problem 4: A Weak Opening and a Weak Ending

The introduction and conclusion often do the most damage, because they're the first and last impression a reader takes away.

Ask of the opening: Does it draw the reader in quickly? Does it get to something real without stalling? Or does it lean on a borrowed quote, a slow "documentary-style" setup, a dictionary definition, or a detail unrelated to the rest of the essay?

Ask of the closing: Does it end on something specific and earned? Does it add new reflection or insight? Or does it just repeat what's already been said, wrapped in vague, abstract statements?

### How to Fix It

When an introduction isn't working, the simplest fix is often the most effective one: cut it entirely and start the essay where the real content begins. Anything essential from the old opening can usually be worked into the new one.

For conclusions, the essay has already earned the right to speak more directly — because the values and qualities being claimed have been demonstrated through the stories already told. Use the ending to reflect on what was learned or how the writer changed, as long as it stays specific and personal rather than sliding into platitudes.

## What May Have Changed Since This Was Written

This advice originated roughly six years ago, in a fast-moving admissions landscape. A few of its specific claims are more time-bound than the rest and are worth re-checking against current information rather than taken at face value:

- **The "1 to 5 outstanding essays per 100" estimate.** This was always presented as an informal consensus among a small group of readers, not a measured statistic. Applicant pools, coaching resources, and essay-writing norms shift over time, so treat this as a rough gut-check rather than a fixed benchmark.
- **How "off-limits" certain topics are.** The idea that mental health, trauma, or divorce are nearly impossible to write about well reflected admissions norms at the time. Attitudes toward disclosing mental health struggles in essays have shifted in various ways across different admissions cycles, and individual schools' receptiveness can vary. If you're drawn to a difficult topic, it's worth getting current, specific feedback rather than ruling it out purely on older general advice.
- **"Admissions has become so competitive."** Competitiveness is not static — acceptance rates, application volumes, and the number of applications per student have all continued to shift year to year (including changes driven by test-optional policies and application platform changes). Whatever the competitive landscape was when this was written, check current admit-rate data for the specific schools you're applying to.
- **Any specific resource links or tools mentioned.** External guides, tools, and blog posts referenced in the original discussion (including the MIT admissions blog) may have been updated, moved, or replaced since publication.

## Golden Rules That Don't Change

Underneath the time-bound specifics, the core craft advice in this piece is durable — it reflects how personal narrative writing works, not a particular admissions cycle:

- **Show, don't tell.** A claim about yourself is only convincing when it's backed by a specific moment, action, or detail — this is a fundamental of narrative writing, not an admissions trend.
- **Specificity beats generality, always.** Concrete, personal detail is what makes writing memorable and hard to copy; vague or universal statements are forgettable no matter when they're read.
- **Authentic voice outperforms impressive-sounding voice.** Readers can tell the difference between someone writing to sound good and someone writing to say something true — this hasn't changed and won't.
- **Cliché is the enemy of memorability.** Phrases that could have been written by any applicant, in any year, do the opposite of what an essay is supposed to do.
- **A single reviewer's opinion is one input, not a verdict.** Feedback is useful for catching real problems, but essay quality is ultimately evaluated in the context of a full, individual application — that will remain true regardless of how admissions trends shift.

## The Bottom Line

None of this requires a dramatic rewrite from scratch. Most essays improve significantly through targeted fixes: sharpening or replacing the topic, cutting what doesn't reveal anything personal, backing up claims with real evidence, and tightening the opening and closing. The goal isn't perfection — it's an essay that sounds like one specific person wrote it, and that a reader remembers after they've read a hundred others. Just hold the era-specific numbers and topic taboos loosely, and check them against current guidance where it matters.
