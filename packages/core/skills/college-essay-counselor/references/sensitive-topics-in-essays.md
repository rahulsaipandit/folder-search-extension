# What Not to Write About in Your College Application — And Where That Advice Is Contested

*Adapted from a widely-discussed post by a college admissions consultant on r/ApplyingToCollege, posted roughly five years ago (2021), plus the debate it generated in the comments.*

A note on scope: this is about essay *strategy*, not a claim about whether students should feel free to experience or discuss mental health struggles in general. The original author is explicit about that distinction, and it's worth preserving here too.

A note on timing: this post is several years old, and it generated real disagreement even at the time — including from two verified admissions officers who landed on opposite sides. Treat the specific claims below as one experienced consultant's read of the landscape circa 2021, not settled fact. Where the underlying logic is likely to hold up regardless of year, and where it's more sensitive to context that can shift, is called out explicitly in the final section.

## The Original Argument: A Hard "No" List

The consultant lists topics he says should never appear in an application, for any reason: clinical depression, anxiety, other mental illness, psychiatric medication, involuntary holds, rage or revenge fantasies, suicidality, interest in mass-casualty weapons, sexual/physical/mental abuse, sexual assault, self-harm, eating disorders, addiction, substance abuse, extreme violence, and gang affiliation. His stated rationale: admissions officers are managing institutional risk (student breakdowns, campus tragedy), and he believes disclosing these topics — however well-written — tends to read as a liability rather than a growth story.

A second, softer tier covers topics he doesn't recommend except in narrow cases: someone else's struggle with the above, learning disabilities, past tragedy or loss, and using any of the banned topics to explain a weak spot in the application (best confined to the Additional Information box, and vaguely worded).

A third tier he considers generally workable with care: hardship from sociopolitical or economic forces (poverty, racism, war, homelessness), LGBTQ identity, and advocacy work connected to these issues.

## The Pushback: This Was Not a Consensus View

The top comment on the post — pinned by a moderator — links to a *different* highly-regarded post on the same forum titled "13 Reasons Why It's OK to Write About Trauma in College Essays." That alone signals this is a live debate within the admissions-advice community, not settled doctrine.

Two verified admissions officers commented, and they disagreed with each other:

- **FeatofClay** (verified former AO) largely agreed with the original post, using an analogy about evaluating a surgeon's readiness regardless of sympathy for what caused a setback in their training.
- **UVaDeanj** (verified current AO) directly disagreed: *"Applicants can absolutely talk about mental health challenges and the story doesn't have to be over at the end of the essay."*

A retired moderator, **McNeilAdmissions**, offered a middle position that several other commenters echoed: *"Any topic can work if written well. Conversely, no topic will work if written poorly."* He noted having worked with students who wrote successfully about sexual assault, learning differences, and family tragedy — while agreeing it's a difficult line to walk without experienced guidance.

Several students offered direct counter-examples:
- One user described writing explicitly about depression and related mental-health research/advocacy work, and being admitted to Harvard and MIT.
- Another described a personal statement that wove mental illness, chronic illness, and identity into a broader narrative — not as the central topic — and was admitted to a top-five liberal arts college, after a *first* application centered squarely on grief and hardship was largely rejected. Her own takeaway: centering an essay on suffering tends not to work, but folding it in as one thread among several can.
- A student who wrote a raw essay about an eating disorder reported admission to UC Berkeley.
- One commenter argued more bluntly that severe, legible hardship (a named disease, a documented tragedy) can actively help an application rather than hurt it, and that the real problem is softer, harder-to-verify topics (introversion, vague "mental health issues") getting overused.

There was also pushback on the advice's real-world *cost*. A few commenters — including one who described eight years of therapy and years of rebuilding self-worth — pointed out that telling a student who has genuinely struggled to "just find another topic" can be much harder than it sounds, and that the advice risks reading as dismissive of students whose lives really have been shaped primarily by hardship, illness, or disability. One reply flagged the framing as carrying an undertone of ableism.

## What Seems to Hold Up Regardless of the Year (Golden Rules)

These principles show up across almost every commenter — including the ones who disagree with the original post's specific list:

1. **Centering an essay on suffering, by itself, rarely works as well as showing growth, agency, or a distinctive way of thinking.** Even commenters who succeeded writing about hard topics say the topic wasn't the reason it worked — the framing, self-awareness, and connection to something forward-looking was.
2. **Word count is genuinely scarce, and whatever you write about becomes your "brand" for that reader.** This is a structural fact about short essays, not an opinion.
3. **A vague, unresolved account of hardship reads worse than either a well-resolved one or a completely different topic.** "I struggled and I'm still struggling" is a much harder essay to land than "I struggled, and here specifically is where I am now."
4. **Difficult topics benefit disproportionately from experienced outside feedback.** Even the commenters most sympathetic to writing about trauma agreed this is a high-difficulty style that's easy to get wrong without a second, honest reader.
5. **You are never obligated to disclose anything in an application.** Choosing not to write about something is always a legitimate option, independent of any admissions trend.
6. **The Additional Information section exists for context that doesn't belong in the personal essay** — it's a lower-risk place to explain circumstances factually without building an entire narrative arc around them.

## What's More Time- and Context-Dependent (Read With Caution)

These are judgment calls that plausibly shift with the admissions cycle, the specific school, or broader cultural attitudes — worth re-checking against current advice rather than taking as fixed:

- **How admissions officers personally weigh mental-health disclosure.** The direct disagreement between two verified AOs in the comments shows this varies by reader, by institution, and likely by year — it is not a fixed industry-wide rule.
- **Which topics colleges are "tired of" versus actively seeking.** Trends in "Essays That Worked" collections, prompts (the post specifically criticizes a since-changed UC prompt), and admissions marketing shift year to year and school to school.
- **The specific claim about admissions officer demographics driving topic preferences.** This is presented as the author's personal read, not verified data, and is the kind of generalization most likely to have aged poorly or been overstated even at the time.
- **Broader cultural stigma around mental health.** Attitudes toward disclosure have been shifting over the past several years (in both directions, and unevenly across institutions), which can change how a given essay lands even if the underlying writing craft is identical.
- **Any statistic or study cited (e.g., the linked "kisses of death" research) is dated to when it was published** and may not reflect the current applicant pool or admissions practices — worth verifying independently if it's going to inform an actual decision.

## The Balanced Takeaway

The strongest, most durable point across both the original post and the pushback isn't "never write about hardship" — it's that **hardship alone isn't an essay strategy; how it's framed, resolved, and balanced against the rest of your application is.** Reasonable, experienced people — including admissions officers themselves — disagree about where exactly that line sits for a given topic. Given that disagreement, the lower-risk path favored by most commenters (even the ones who succeeded writing about hard topics) is: don't center an entire personal statement on unresolved struggle, get a second opinion before submitting anything on a sensitive topic, and remember that choosing *not* to disclose something is always a legitimate, complete strategy on its own.
