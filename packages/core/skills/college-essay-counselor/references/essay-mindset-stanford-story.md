# How I Got Into Stanford With a 1440 SAT, No Subject Tests, and Unremarkable Extracurriculars

*A South Korean international student's college essay strategy went viral on Reddit's r/ApplyingToCollege — here's the story, and what critics said about it.*

*Note on timing: This post appears to date to around 2020 — commenters reference the current admissions cycle amid COVID and cite Stanford's 2018 financial aid data. That's roughly six years old at the time of this writing. Some of what follows is timeless; some of it reflects a specific, now-dated admissions landscape. Both are flagged below.*

When a recent high school graduate posted their Stanford admissions story on r/ApplyingToCollege, it struck a nerve. The headline alone — getting into one of the most selective universities in the world with a 1440 SAT score, zero SAT Subject Tests, and what they called "mediocre" extracurriculars — drew hundreds of upvotes and dozens of comments within days.

The student, who applied only to Stanford and the University of Chicago in the U.S. (getting into the former and waitlisted at the latter), was upfront that their experience might not generalize. "Everyone's under their own circumstances, so what worked for me may not for you," they wrote, encouraging readers to weigh their story against the pushback in the comments section.

## The Numbers

By the student's own account, their application had some obvious weak points for an Ivy-League-adjacent reach school:

- **1440 SAT** (with a 670 on the English section — below Stanford's 25th percentile, and a 17 on the essay)
- **0 SAT Subject Tests**
- **0 honors or awards**
- **No art portfolio**
- **A modest extracurricular record**, topped by a five-hour-a-week high school writing club

But one number stood out: a **44 out of 45 on the IB diploma**, up from 39/45 the previous year. The student, a South Korean citizen with no U.S. citizenship or green card, attended an international school in Singapore, applied without financial aid, didn't interview, and had no legacy status.

## What They Think Made the Difference

The poster was careful to frame their reasoning as speculation — they never saw their actual admissions file — but offered a few theories.

**A standout academic record.** A 44/45 IB score, they noted, put them near the top of their graduating class of 330 students, even if their standardized test scores lagged. "You have wiggle room for 'flaws'" once your transcript clears a high bar, they argued.

**Strong recommendation letters** from teachers who, in the student's view, understood them well enough to speak to qualities like resilience and self-awareness rather than just academic performance.

**Essays that did the heavy lifting.** This was the crux of the post. Applying to only two schools let the student focus intensely on a small number of essays, and they credited two books — *The Elements of Style* by Strunk and White, and William Zinsser's *On Writing Well* — with teaching them to write "concisely with humanity and warmth." Notably, every Stanford admit receives a short personal note from their regional admissions officer after acceptance, and the student said theirs specifically praised their writing.

Their advice to other applicants: read those two books repeatedly, then get honest feedback from someone who doesn't already know you. "If your essay isn't interesting to someone who doesn't know you, it won't be interesting to the AO who's piling through hundreds of essays every day."

**A less common applicant profile.** The student speculated, with visible uncertainty, that being a Korean student interested in English and creative writing — rather than a more heavily represented field like STEM — may have made their application stand out simply through relative rarity. They were careful to frame this as an observation rather than a strategy, explicitly cautioning other students against fabricating an interest in a field like Korean studies just to seem different if their real passion lay elsewhere.

**Caring less, or at least caring differently.** Perhaps the most emphasized point in the post was mindset. The student described deciding to apply to Stanford only about four weeks before the regular decision deadline, treating it as "more of a hail mary than a genuine attempt." That lower-stakes framing, they said, let them write essays based on what felt personally meaningful rather than what they guessed an admissions officer wanted to hear — including a line directly addressing their reader: if you're focused on my grades or resume, they wrote, you're missing the point of who I am.

## The Pushback

Not every reader was convinced the story told the whole picture. One commenter, describing themselves as a fellow Singapore-based international student, pushed back thoughtfully in a widely upvoted reply.

They pointed out that a 1440 SAT is still roughly the 96th percentile nationally, and that the post's clickbait-y framing undersold the student's actual academic strength. More pointedly, they raised the question of financial privilege: citing Stanford's own aid data showing that a large majority of international students at the time paid full price (in the tens of thousands of dollars per year), the commenter argued that any full-pay international applicant — including this student, who didn't apply for financial aid — was already competing from a position of relative advantage, whether or not personal connections were involved. They also gently critiqued the essay device of "breaking the fourth wall" to address the admissions officer directly, warning it can come off as tacky if not handled carefully, and noted that ultimately, even admissions officers say they can't fully explain individual decisions — a reminder that any single applicant's postmortem is inherently incomplete.

The original poster responded graciously, agreeing that their IB score was genuinely strong even if their SAT wasn't, and acknowledging they had no way to fully rule out the role of circumstance in their outcome.

## What's Timeless vs. What's Dated

Because this account is several years old, it's worth separating the parts of it that hold up regardless of when you're reading this from the parts tied to a specific moment in admissions history.

**Likely to change or already outdated:**

- **The testing landscape.** In 2020, the SAT was still near-universal and test-optional policies were just beginning (many schools, including Stanford, adopted them partly *because of* COVID disruptions). A 1440 read very differently in an admissions cycle where almost everyone submitted scores than it does in an era of widespread test-optional or test-blind policies. Whether a score like this "matters less" today depends entirely on current policy at the specific school — that's worth checking directly rather than assuming.
- **The financial aid statistics.** The critical comment cites Stanford's 2018 aid data (roughly 29% of international students receiving aid, at a specific average package size). Aid budgets, endowment payouts, and the share of full-pay international students shift year to year and shouldn't be treated as current.
- **Acceptance rates and applicant pool size.** Selectivity at schools like Stanford has generally tightened over time as application volumes rise (partly due to the Common App and test-optional policies increasing applications per student). Whatever the odds were in this applicant's year, they are not this year's odds.
- **"Uncommon major" advantages.** The idea that being an English-focused applicant from a country associated with STEM applicants made this student stand out is plausible for a specific cycle at a specific school, but application trends by demographic and intended major shift over time and by institution. This is circumstantial, not a durable strategy.

**Likely to hold up regardless of year:**

- **Write with specificity and honesty, not for an imagined committee.** Essays that reflect genuine reflection over guessing what "sounds good" are consistently cited by admissions offices, across eras, as the differentiator in a strong application.
- **Get outside, honest feedback on your writing** — ideally from someone who doesn't already know your context, so you learn whether the writing itself lands.
- **A single weak metric rarely sinks an otherwise strong, coherent application** at schools that genuinely practice holistic review — but a single strong metric (like a 44/45 IB score) rarely guarantees admission either, as the poster themselves noted, citing perfect scorers at their own school who were rejected.
- **No one — including the applicant — can fully reconstruct why a specific decision was made.** This was true in 2020 and remains true today; admissions offices don't disclose the weighting of individual factors, and outcomes involve enough context (institutional priorities, applicant pool composition in a given year, financial considerations) that any single account is necessarily partial.
- **Fit and self-acceptance regarding outcome.** The framing that "where you go is not who you'll be" isn't a hedge specific to 2020 — it's the piece of advice that ages best precisely because it doesn't depend on any particular year's admissions statistics.

The practical implication: read this post's writing advice and mindset advice as durable. Read anything involving specific percentages, policies, or "this is how admissions works right now" as a snapshot of one cycle — verify against current data before treating it as guidance for today's applications.

## If You're Applying Today: Where to Check Current Policy

Given how much the testing and aid landscape has shifted since this post was written, don't rely on this article — or any single Reddit post — for current specifics. Go to primary sources directly:

- **Testing policy (test-optional, test-blind, or required):** Check the admissions page of each specific school you're applying to. Policies vary by institution and have changed multiple times in recent years, so a school's own current-cycle announcement is the only reliable source — third-party aggregators and older forum posts can be out of date within a single admissions cycle.
- **Financial aid, including for international students:** Go to the school's financial aid office page rather than aid statistics quoted in old posts or comments (like the 2018 figures cited in this thread). Look specifically for whether the school is need-blind or need-aware *for international applicants* — this differs from domestic policy at many schools, including some Ivy-Plus institutions.
- **Acceptance rates and applicant pool data:** Schools typically publish a Common Data Set annually (searchable by school name + "Common Data Set") with figures more granular and current than headline acceptance-rate news coverage.
- **Application requirements generally:** The Common App or Coalition App portal reflects each member school's current-year requirements directly, and is more reliable than secondhand summaries.

The core advice on essay-writing and mindset in this article doesn't require this kind of verification — it's not time-bound. Anything involving numbers, percentages, or "here's how it works" claims should be checked against this year's official sources before you act on it.

## The Takeaway

Stripped of the specifics, the post's core argument is a familiar one in admissions discourse, but delivered with more candor than usual: that a single glaring weakness — in this case, test scores — isn't necessarily disqualifying if the rest of an application, particularly the writing, is distinctive and honest. The student was quick to note that this isn't a loophole so much as a description of how "holistic" admissions is supposed to work in the first place.

Still, as the comment thread illustrated, no single account can capture why an admissions committee says yes. Strong grades, a compelling essay, geographic and demographic context, financial circumstances, and plain luck all likely play some role — and disentangling them from the outside is close to impossible, even for the applicant themselves.

As the student put it, closing their post with a nod to writer Frank Bruni: "Where you go is not who you'll be."
