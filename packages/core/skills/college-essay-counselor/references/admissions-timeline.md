# The College Admissions Journey Timeline: A Guide for Rising Juniors and Seniors

*Adapted from a widely shared guide by a private admissions consultant and former moderator of r/ApplyingToCollege, originally posted in May 2020 ("Updated Spring 2020"). The comment thread that followed added useful pushback and nuance from other students and readers, which is folded in below.*

## A note on timing before you read this

This guide is now several years old, written in the middle of the COVID-19 disruption to admissions. Two things follow from that:

1. **The general philosophy holds up well.** Advice about course rigor, genuine extracurricular involvement, financial fit, and managing stress isn't really time-bound — it reflects how holistic admissions offices have worked for a long time and likely will continue to work.
2. **Specific policies, tools, and numbers age fast.** Testing requirements, which platforms colleges use, which websites still exist, and which schools are test-optional all shift year to year — sometimes year over year at the *same* school. Treat anything in this category as a starting point for your own research, not a final answer.

To help with that, this version marks specific claims that are more likely to have changed with a ⚠️, and closes with a section of principles that are safe to treat as durable regardless of when you're reading this. A round of web research (current as of late August 2026, sourced from admissions-advising sites and the platforms' own pages) has been folded in throughout to correct or update the parts of the original that no longer reflect how things work. Policy in this space moves fast even year to year, so treat the 2026 specifics below the same way you'd treat the 2020 original: a snapshot, not a permanent answer — confirm anything time-sensitive against the college's own current page before acting on it.

## Rising Junior Summer (Summer Before Junior Year)

**Enjoy your summer.** This matters more than it sounds. Recharging is part of taking care of yourself, so take real time off from school and admissions altogether.

**Choose activities that are actually yours.** As MIT Admissions puts it: *"Choose your activities because they delight, intrigue, and challenge you, not because you think they'll look impressive on your application... College is not a costume party; you're not supposed to come dressed as someone else."* Some students thrive doing a lot; others do best focused on just a few things. Either way, the test is simple: does it make you happy?

**Get involved — with yourself, your family, and your community.**
- *Yourself:* build healthy habits, pursue personal projects, read real books, get a job, learn something new, practice mindfulness.
- *Family:* help with siblings or grandparents, take on a regular chore, cook dinner once a week.
- *Community:* service doesn't have to be a formal program — play music at a retirement home, coach a kids' team, volunteer at a food bank.

**Get a summer job.** An ordinary job — scooping ice cream, folding sweaters, whatever it is — teaches accountability to a boss and customers in a way unpaid internships often don't.

**Start your testing prep.** Testing policy has shifted substantially since 2020 — here's where things actually stand as of the 2026–2027 cycle:
- Practice for the **PSAT** (taken in October; a strong score can qualify you for National Merit).
- Take practice tests on the **Digital SAT** (now the only format — fully computer-adaptive, about 2 hours 14 minutes total, taken through the College Board's Bluebook app with a built-in Desmos calculator) and the **ACT**, then commit to one. Because the SAT format itself has changed, older paper-based prep materials and pacing advice are less useful than they used to be — look for prep built specifically for the digital, adaptive format.
- **Test-optional is no longer the default at the top of the selectivity range.** MIT, Dartmouth, Yale, Brown, Georgetown, Harvard, Caltech, Stanford, Princeton, and Columbia have all reinstated mandatory testing for undergraduate applicants. Meanwhile, hundreds of other schools remain test-optional or test-blind. This is genuinely a per-school question now — check each target school's current policy directly rather than assuming either way.
- Superscoring and score-choice are still the norm at most schools, but a handful of the schools above (Georgetown, notably) require you to submit your *entire* testing history rather than picking your best sitting — another reason to check policy per school before you register for repeat tests.
- ~~Consider SAT Subject Tests~~ — **this no longer applies, with no ambiguity.** The College Board permanently discontinued SAT Subject Tests in 2021 and has no plans to bring them back. AP exams have effectively taken over that role for demonstrating subject-specific strength. The optional SAT Essay is also gone for standard test dates — it now only appears where a state has mandated it as part of school-day testing, so most students will never see it.

**Start your resume.** List everything you've done since freshman year. Anything outside of class time and homework counts. Organize it into categories: Education, Extracurriculars, Work Experience, Community Service, Interests and Hobbies, Awards, Honors.

**Start exploring admissions.** Read college admissions blogs and websites for candid, first-hand context on the process. RaiseMe is still active as of 2026, with 300+ partner colleges offering micro-scholarships for logged high school achievements starting as early as 9th grade — it's a low-effort way to build a paper trail of interest and effort over time.

> **On RaiseMe and international students:** a commenter on the original post asked whether this tool is worth using as an international applicant. The honest answer, per other commenters: it's worth trying, but it requires attending an American-style school, many partner colleges don't offer aid to international students through it, and the scholarships it generates are unlikely to offset full financial need on their own. It's a low-cost way to demonstrate interest, not a financial aid strategy.

## Junior Fall

Everything from the summer still applies. In addition:

**Take a rigorous course load, evaluated in the context of what your school actually offers.** Colleges weigh rigor heavily, and a commonly cited target is four years each of:
- Science (Biology, Chemistry, Physics)
- Foreign language
- English
- Math (through calculus, if offered)
- Social science

**A necessary caveat from the comment thread:** several students pushed back on the "four years of language" guidance, and the original poster's own answers there are worth reading directly:
- The four-years recommendation is aimed at applicants to highly selective schools; it is *not* a universal requirement, and plenty of great schools don't need it.
- If dropping a language to make room for something you're more invested in (a music class, an extra science elective) is genuinely the better choice for you, check the *specific* recommended/required coursework listed on the websites of colleges you're targeting rather than assuming a fixed rule applies. Requirements vary by school.
- Being bilingual already, or having strong reasons tied to your intended major, are legitimate factors to weigh — this isn't a one-size-fits-all rule.

**Build relationships with your teachers.** Visit office hours, speak up in class, ask for help. You'll be asking these teachers for recommendations soon.

**Keep your grades up — but keep perspective.** One A- or B won't sink your chances, and there are excellent colleges looking for B students. That said, a commenter reasonably pushed back on this being read as "grades barely matter" — for the most selective schools in particular, even very strong grades and scores are no guarantee, a point the original poster confirmed directly in the thread. The honest takeaway: don't panic over small dips, but don't mistake this for a signal that rigor and GPA don't matter at the top end.

**Read constantly.** Fiction, nonfiction, magazines, newspapers — reading strengthens both test scores and essay writing.

**Continue ACT/SAT prep**, aiming to start actual test dates in late fall or early spring.

**Stay involved**, and check in with your high school counselor about how the process works at your specific school — every school's procedures differ.

## Junior Spring

**Line up your letters of recommendation.** Ask two or three junior-year teachers in core academic subjects — ideally one STEM and one humanities — who know you well. Include a resume and a short answer sheet covering your favorite part of the class, a memorable project, what you found difficult, and what you'll carry forward. Send a thank-you note afterward.

**Start visiting colleges** — in person if possible, virtually if not. Go on the official tour, but also wander independently, talk to students, and check out spaces the tour skips.

**Begin your college list**, using a mix of official sources (college websites, the Common Data Set) and outside perspective (guidebooks, current-student social media, forums). Cast a wide net at first; you'll narrow naturally.

**Identify a Sure-Fire Safety School** — one where your stats guarantee admission, that's an actual financial fit, and that you'd genuinely be happy attending. Note that holistic-admission schools don't count as safeties until you're actually admitted.

**Think seriously about fit, not just prestige**, including financial need, geography, campus culture, size, and how your academic profile compares to each school's typical admitted student.

**Set a no-college-talk zone or time** at home. This isn't a small thing — constant college talk for a year straight is hard on everyone.

**Do net price calculators together as a family**, using the calculator on each college's own site, and start the harder financial conversations early.

> **On financial aid strategy, from the comment thread — and updated with current specifics:** one detailed reply is worth summarizing directly, since it adds real nuance the original post doesn't fully cover:
> - Most highly selective U.S. schools remain need-blind for domestic applicants but need-aware for international ones. As of 2026, only a small tier — Harvard, Princeton, Yale, MIT, Amherst, Bowdoin, and Dartmouth among them — extends need-blind admissions to international students too. Outside that short list, assume need-aware for international applications.
> - A commonly cited (if debated) strategy: avoid proactively requesting aid consideration until after admission, at schools where doing so won't affect the decision. This depends entirely on whether a given school is genuinely need-blind for *your* applicant type (domestic vs. international) — verify this per school rather than assuming it.
> - Some colleges **reduce your institutional aid package if you bring in outside scholarships** ("scholarship displacement") — but this practice is less common than it used to be. Several states (California, Maryland, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Washington among them) have passed laws restricting or banning it. It still happens elsewhere to avoid awarding more than the total cost of attendance, so check each target school's policy before assuming outside scholarships are risk-free.

**Create a college-specific email address**, and check its spam folder regularly.

**Start practicing your writing** — not the essay itself yet, just writing regularly and reading personal essays for voice, not structure. A common piece of advice worth repeating: avoid reading "essays that got in," since it tends to produce imitative, formulaic writing rather than an authentic one.

> **One landscape shift worth knowing about:** following the 2023 U.S. Supreme Court ruling ending race-conscious affirmative action in admissions, personal essays have taken on more weight, not less. Colleges can no longer consider race as a checkbox, but they can and do read essays closely for how an applicant's background, identity, or experience overcoming adversity shaped who they are — so an authentic, specific personal essay matters at least as much now as when the original guide was written, arguably more. Separately, public scrutiny has pushed a number of institutions to scale back or eliminate legacy admissions preferences, though this varies by school and is worth checking directly if it's relevant to you.

**Manage the stress deliberately.** Stay involved in things you enjoy for their own sake, practice gratitude, let go of what you can't control, and don't suppress anxiety about the process — it's a normal part of it. Simple, short mindfulness practice and physical movement help more than people expect.

**If you're a low-income applicant**, look into programs like QuestBridge, the Jack Kent Cooke Foundation Scholarship, and The Posse Foundation — all three remain active and well-regarded as of 2026, with eligibility still focused on low-income, high-achieving, and leadership-oriented students. QuestBridge's National College Match deadline generally lands in late September or early October; confirm the exact date each cycle, since it can shift by a few days year to year.

## Rising Senior Summer

The same principles from rising junior summer apply. Research, internships, structured programs, independent projects, and ordinary summer jobs are all valid uses of time — there's no single right answer, and unstructured time to rest has real value too.

**Finish standardized testing** where possible.

**Recharge deliberately** — movement, rest, connection.

**Start writing toward your personal statement**, using open-ended prompts (What do you worry about? Who are your role models and why? What's the last thing you think about before falling asleep?) as a way to find material and voice before worrying about structure.

**Set up your application platform accounts.** This part of the original guide is genuinely outdated: the standalone Coalition Application ("MyCoalition") has been discontinued. The Coalition for College now applies exclusively through the Scoir platform, which has grown into the main alternative to the Common App — the two now even integrate directly, letting counselors sync documents across both systems. In practice, this means most students should expect to set up a **Common App** account (still the dominant platform) and check whether any target schools require applying through **Scoir** instead of, or alongside, it. The Common App profile-rollover behavior from the original guide still works as described: your Profile, Family, Education, and Activities sections carry over automatically each August 1st when the new cycle opens.

**Update your resume, continue visiting colleges, and start narrowing your list**, making sure it includes at least one genuine Sure-Fire Safety School.

**Stay in touch with colleges** — request info, follow admissions offices, and reach out to your regional admissions officer with real questions.

## Senior Fall

**Finish college visits**, especially for Early Decision candidates.

**Narrow your list**, build a tracking spreadsheet of deadlines and requirements, and finish the baseline sections of your applications.

**Apply Early Action** wherever it's available and makes sense for you.

**Start financial aid paperwork** (FAFSA, and CSS Profile where required), and reach out to financial aid offices directly.

**Follow up with your recommenders**, check in with your counselor, finish remaining testing, and submit scores with enough lead time before deadlines — checking each school's self-reporting policy.

**Write your essays**, starting with the personal statement (a reasonable target is having it done by October 1), then working through supplements by due date.

**Take interviews seriously** when offered, even if optional — and check spam folders for invitations.

**If deferred Early Decision**, a Letter of Continued Interest can help if you're still genuinely interested.

---

## Senior Spring

**Finish up applications** — many colleges still accept them well into spring.

**Confirm your safety school** is locked in.

**Protect your grades.** Rescinded offers over grade drops do happen, though an occasional dip isn't catastrophic.

**Prioritize your mental health** during the waiting period — it's genuinely one of the harder parts of the process.

**Manage expectations**, stay grounded in your safety school, and remember that even strong candidates get rejected from selective schools for reasons that have nothing to do with their worth as a student.

**Practice gratitude**, and thank the people who helped along the way.

**If waitlisted**, a Letter of Continued Interest can help.

## What's changed since the 2020 original (confirmed as of August 2026)

These were the parts of the guide most likely to be outdated. Here's the current state of each — and a note on which of these could still shift again by the time you're reading this:

- **SAT Subject Tests are gone for good.** Discontinued by the College Board in 2021, with no plans to reinstate them. This one is settled; you can stop wondering about it.
- **The SAT itself has changed format.** It's now fully digital and adaptive (about 2 hours 14 minutes total), taken through the College Board's Bluebook app. Older prep materials built for the paper SAT are less useful now.
- **Test-optional is no longer a safe default assumption at selective schools.** MIT, Dartmouth, Yale, Brown, Georgetown, Harvard, Caltech, Stanford, Princeton, and Columbia have all reinstated required testing as of 2026. Hundreds of other schools remain test-optional or test-blind. ⚠️ *This is the one item on this list still most likely to move again* — testing policy has already reversed once industry-wide, so check each target school's current-cycle policy directly rather than trusting any static list, including this one.
- **The Coalition App as a standalone platform is gone.** It now runs through Scoir, which has become the main alternative to Common App. Expect to use Common App as your primary platform, with Scoir required by some schools.
- **RaiseMe, FairTest, and Fastweb/CollegeScholarships.com are all still active and legitimate** as of 2026. The "2021admissions.org" site referenced in the original is outdated — FairTest's own directory is the better current source for test-optional/test-blind school lists.
- **Scholarship displacement is less common than it was**, thanks to state-level restrictions in several states, but it hasn't disappeared everywhere — still worth confirming per school.
- **Post-affirmative-action, essays and identity carry more explicit weight**, and legacy preferences have been scaled back at a number of schools (though not eliminated everywhere).
- **Specific deadlines, required coursework, and school-by-school financial aid policy** are still set by each institution and reviewed annually — nothing above replaces checking a given school's own current admissions page before you rely on it.

## Appendix: Template Email for Asking a Financial Aid Office About Outside Scholarships

Given the scholarship displacement nuance covered above, it's worth asking each school directly rather than assuming a policy either way. Here's a template to adapt:

> **Subject:** Inquiry Regarding Outside Scholarship Policy – [Student's Full Name]
>
> Dear Financial Aid Office,
>
> I am writing to clarify your institution's financial aid policies regarding outside awards for the upcoming academic year. I am an applicant for the upcoming cycle, and I am actively applying to private, external scholarships to help fund my education.
>
> To help my family plan effectively, could you please clarify how outside scholarships impact an institutional financial aid package? Specifically:
>
> - **Scholarship Displacement:** If I receive an outside scholarship, does your office use those funds to reduce the self-help portion of my financial aid package (such as student loans or work-study), or does it reduce the institutional grant aid first?
> - **Cost of Attendance:** If the total amount of my outside awards exceeds my calculated student contribution, does your office allow these funds to cover additional educational expenses, such as a computer, health insurance, or travel costs?
> - **Reporting Timeline:** What is the deadline and preferred method for reporting external scholarship wins to your office?
>
> Thank you very much for your time and for helping my family navigate the financial planning process.
>
> Sincerely,
> [Student's Full Name]
> [Applicant ID Number, if applicable]
> [Student Phone Number]
> [Student Email Address]

## Principles that hold up regardless of when you're reading this

- **Course rigor is judged in context.** Colleges look at what your school actually offers, not a fixed national checklist.
- **Genuine involvement reads better than strategic involvement.** Activities chosen because you care about them tend to produce better essays, better recommendations, and a better four years, independent of any admissions calculus.
- **A real safety school is not optional.** It has to be a place you'd actually be glad to attend and can actually afford — not just a fallback in name only.
- **Financial fit matters as much as academic or cultural fit.** Running the numbers early, as a family, avoids a lot of pain later.
- **Relationships with teachers and counselors are built over time, not requested at the last minute.** Start early enough that a recommendation reflects real familiarity.
- **Reading and regular writing practice pay off across the entire process** — testing, essays, and interviews alike.
- **Taking care of your mental health during this process is not a luxury.** Stress and disappointment are a normal part of a process this competitive; they aren't a sign you're doing something wrong.
- **When in doubt, check the specific college's current official page, or ask your counselor** — general guides (including this one) are a starting point, not a substitute for that.

## The Short Version

Junior year is when real admissions prep begins, and there's more time than it feels like. Take the philosophy of this guide seriously, verify the specifics against current sources, check in with your counselor along the way, and prioritize figuring out what genuinely interests you over what you think looks good on paper.
